# A Geometric Framework for Pitch Estimation on Acoustic Musical Signals (arXiv:2012.04517, preprint Nov 2020) — Tom Goodman, Karoline van Gemst, Peter Tino (Univ. of Birmingham / Sheffield)

**Relevance to SAO: off-lane — a combinatorial/group-theoretic pitch-transcription toy model, not persistent homology/TDA, not competitive with existing pitch/chroma extraction, and not validated on real polyphonic (multi-instrument/dense-mix) audio. No actionable mechanism for LatCH control, feature extraction, or eval metrics.**
**Status: DEEP-READ (full PDF, 2026-08-12, GHOST-NOTE).**

The paper reframes mono-/multi-pitch estimation (MPE) as a discrete combinatorics problem on a grid, not as a topological-data-analysis (persistent homology) method — worth flagging up front since it was assigned as part of a topology/geometry-of-music sweep. There is no simplicial complex, no persistence diagram, no Betti number anywhere in the paper; "geometric" here means "laid out on a 2D/3D grid with a cyclic group acting on it," in the lineage of Euler's Tonnetz and Tymoczko's *A Geometry of Music* (both cited and discussed at length in §2, including OPTIC transformations and the two-note-chord-space Möbius strip — none of which the paper's own model actually uses beyond inspiration).

**Mechanism.** Define the grid $\mathcal{N}^{\Sigma} = \chi^{\Sigma} \times \mathbb{Z}$, where $\chi^{\Sigma}$ is the 12 pitch chromas ordered by the circle of fifths (not chromatically) and $\mathbb{Z}$ is pitch height (octave). A note is a cell $\nu_{i,j}$; an "interpretation" $\mathcal{I}_\tau: \mathcal{N}^\Sigma \to \mathbb{B}$ marks which cells are audible at time slice $\tau$ (extended to $\mathbb{R}$-valued amplitude for the real-audio case in §8). Two generators, $\delta$ (shift by a fifth) and $\omega$ (shift up an octave), generate a $\mathbb{Z}_{12}\times\mathbb{Z}$ group action on the grid. The key empirical/structural claim: because the circle-of-fifths ordering is used (not chromatic order), a fundamental plus its first three harmonics ($f_0, f_1=2f_0, f_2=3f_0, f_3=4f_0$) always forms one of exactly two spatially-compact shapes — a "turnstile" (⊢) or "gamma" (Γ) — depending on which of the 12 chromas the fundamental sits on (a 5-element vs 7-element split of the 12 pitch classes). This is demonstrated visually on real spectral heatmaps of flute/piano/trumpet/violin (Fig. 19) and on a Bach chorale piano-roll reconstruction (Fig. 20), and does hold up in those illustrations.

The paper's actual algorithmic content is a taxonomy of **false fundamentals** ("edge cases" — harmonics of a real note that happen to also look like a valid ⊢/Γ pattern and would be misclassified as their own fundamental by a naive shape-matching detector). Using the group structure, they formally enumerate all "basic edge cases" (a false fundamental where each of its harmonics has exactly one possible generator) and prove there are exactly **24 basic edge types**, falling into 8 canonical "types" up to a $\delta \mapsto \omega^k\delta$ equivalence (Table 2, Prop 5.1, Cor 5.1.1: no 5-fundamental basic edge case is possible). They then do a Monte Carlo prevalence study (1000–20000 random synthetic "interpretations" of the full theoretical note space, not real music) showing 3-fundamental edge cases dominate at low polyphony and 4-fundamental cases (~3.6%) become non-negligible only above ~16 simultaneous fundamentals — but explicitly caveat that "it is hard to directly relate these results to real-world data ... which is much more structured than the random samples that were used" (p. xxii).

**Empirical results (real numbers, monophonic only):** On 1395 monophonic samples from the University of Iowa Electronic Music Studios dataset (17 instruments, vibrato/non-vibrato), their "naive algorithm" (classify every ⊢/Γ-shaped note as a fundamental, using a CQT with 4096-sample window / 1024 hop, binned to 120 semitone bins C0–B9) gets **73.74% overall accuracy, 88.27% excluding outlier instruments (bowed/pizzicato strings, tuba), 95.08% chroma-only accuracy** (octave errors ignored) — versus a from-scratch implementation of Noll's 1970 Harmonic Product Spectrum (HPS) benchmark at **58.36% / 67.73% / 77.61%** respectively (Table 4; per-instrument breakdown in Appendix B, Table B1). So the naive geometric shape-matcher beats their own HPS baseline by a wide margin, but HPS is a 50-year-old low-bar reference, not a modern MPE system (no comparison to CREPE, HMM/deep-learning transcription baselines like the Elowsson/Kelz work they cite in §2, or actual numbers on the standard benchmarks they name-drop — MAPS, Bach10, TRIOS, MIREX — despite discussing them at length in related work). **For polyphony**, §9's "simple polyphonic algorithm" (traverse the grid, treat lowest-amplitude-thresholded note as fundamental, discard subsequent notes whose harmonics overlap ≥2 already-classified fundamentals) is evaluated **only against synthetic simulated interpretations** (Fig. 24, same Monte Carlo sampling as §7), never against a real polyphonic recording or a labeled polyphonic dataset — accuracy there is a smooth theoretical curve (~65–100% depending on the number of simultaneous fundamentals, worst around 50–75 simultaneous notes), not a reported benchmark number.

**What it does NOT contain:**
- No persistent homology, no simplicial/cell complexes, no topological invariants (Betti numbers, homology groups) — despite "geometric" in the title, the actual math is a finite cyclic group action on a grid (closer to Tonnetz/Tymoczko-style geometric music theory than to TDA). If the SAO reading sweep's goal is specifically PH-informed control/eval methods, this paper is not that literature.
- No real-audio polyphonic benchmark. The polyphonic algorithm is validated only on synthetic combinatorial samples of the idealized note-space, never on an actual multi-instrument recording, stem mix, or a labeled dataset (MAPS/Bach10/MIREX are cited as related work but never run against).
- No learned/trainable component, no differentiability, no GPU implementation — everything is a hand-built combinatorial/rule-based classifier over a CQT-derived heatmap. There's nothing here pluggable as a torch module or a loss term.
- No treatment of timbre, onset, rhythm, or any non-pitch musical attribute — strictly single-purpose pitch/fundamental classification.
- No noise-robustness story beyond a hand-picked amplitude threshold heuristic ($3.25\mu$, from their own earlier paper Goodman & Batten 2018); they explicitly note real instruments (piano, violin, bowed strings) degrade the clean ⊢/Γ shape due to sympathetic resonance and multi-source spectral leakage, and that inharmonic-noise removal is "beyond the scope of this paper."
- The authors themselves are explicit and repeated: "though this simple approach does not reach state of the art results" (§9) and the whole thing is meant as "a solid foundation... not state of the art" (§10) — a proof-of-concept combinatorial taxonomy, not a competitive pitch tracker.

**Verdict for SAO:** the project already has far more mature, validated pitch/chroma extraction in active use (mir's HPCP/`chroma_linmap` NNLS, MERT embeddings, essentia/madmom pipelines feeding the whole-track timeseries and the LatCH chroma head). This paper's shape-taxonomy is a neat piece of combinatorics/group theory for classical harmonic-series false-positive reasoning, illustrated cleanly on clean solo-instrument recordings, but it is untested on real polyphonic/dense mixes (exactly SAO's Goa/psytrance domain), has no trainable/differentiable form, and underperforms in absolute terms next to what's already wired into the pipeline. Not worth porting as a feature extractor or eval metric; filed as a conceptual cross-reference only (the ⊢/Γ shape-on-circle-of-fifths idea could in principle inspire a cheap non-learned sanity-check for chroma-authority evals, but nothing in the paper gets you closer to that than the mir tooling already does).
