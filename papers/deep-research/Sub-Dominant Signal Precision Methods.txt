Numerical Precision Requirements for Sub-Dominant Signal Representation and Learning in Diffusion Transformers
1. System Architecture and the Sub-Dominant Signal Paradox
The deployment of large-scale rectified-flow diffusion transformers (DiTs) has revolutionized generative modeling. However, the internal optimization dynamics of these networks reveal profound vulnerabilities when dealing with highly anisotropic latent spaces. The system under analysis—a 1.4-billion parameter DiT operating as the core of Stable Audio 3 (medium)—functions within the latent space of a frozen SAME autoencoder characterized by a 256-dimensional latent space at 10.77 Hz, achieving a 4096× compression ratio1. Within this mathematically dense environment, the signal corresponding to musical "melody" manifests as an extreme sub-dominant feature. Empirical measurements demonstrate that this melody subspace is distributed across approximately 15 dimensions, holding a mere 9.3% of the total corpus variance, which represents a 1.6× enrichment over random noise1.
The fundamental complexity of this system lies in its severe anisotropy. The latent covariance is 786× anisotropic, resulting in 188 of the 256 eigendirections residing strictly below the unit flow-target noise floor1. The participation ratio is exceedingly low, sitting at approximately 37/2561. Because the master weights, gradients, optimizer states, and explicit normalizations are maintained in 32-bit floating-point (fp32) precision, while the computationally intensive attention core operates dynamically in 16-bit precision (casting fp32 to fp16 for query-key and attention-value matrix multiplications via a Flash Attention kernel), a critical representational gap emerges1.
The central paradox is that standard numerical precision paradigms—such as fp16 and bfloat16 (bf16)—are inherently optimized to preserve the dominant modes of a distribution. When a precision-sensitive delta (  ) resides beneath the standard measurement and operational noise floor, standard mixed-precision training mechanisms actively suppress or irreversibly round away the information necessary to represent and learn the signal. Resolving this requires an exhaustive evaluation of forward representation fidelity, gradient noise during weight updates, the unique confounds of interconnected low-precision attention networks, and the implementation of differential testing to observe below-floor phenomena.

3. Forward Representation Fidelity and Information-Theoretic Bounds

When hardware executes fp16 or bf16 quantization uniformly across a highly anisotropic 256-dimensional tensor where 188 dimensions fall below the flow-target noise floor, the hardware is inadvertently executing a localized form of reverse water-filling1. The precision bits are monopolized by the dominant spatial and structural frequencies. Because the 15-dimensional melody subspace holds only 9.3% of the variance, a standard fp16 forward pass operates with a quantization noise floor that likely swallows these specific dimensions.
3.2 Information Geometry and Directional SQNR
To accurately measure forward representation capability, standard Euclidean SQNR must be discarded in favor of directional sensitivity metrics. Standard quantization metrics treat quantization errors as isotropic perturbations, failing to capture the geometric relationship between the loss function's sensitivity and the direction of the error12.
Recent advancements in information geometry, specifically the Riemannian Sensitivity-Aware Vector Quantization (RSAVQ) framework, model the parameter space of the neural network as a Riemannian manifold possessing non-uniform curvature13. This curvature is precisely described by the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM)13. RSAVQ introduces Error Direction Sensitivity Guidance (EDSG), a methodology that leverages the FIM-induced Riemannian metric to project quantization errors away from highly sensitive areas13.
By projecting the quantization error onto the negative natural gradient direction, researchers can accurately assess how perturbations along specific axes affect the global loss12. For the DiT in question, calculating the per-tensor SQNR is insufficient. The fp16 quantization noise vector must be projected directly onto the known Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) melody eigenbasis. If the resulting error vector exhibits high cosine similarity with the melody subspace, the forward fp16 precision is actively destructive to the sub-dominant signal.
4. Gradient Noise, Optimizer States, and Weight Learning
While forward representation ensures the signal exists, the crux of the sub-dominant signal challenge is a dynamic training issue: ensuring the gradient signal that trains the melody subspace exceeds the rounding noise during the optimizer's weight update.
4.1 The Threat of Gradient Underflow in Mixed Precision
Mixed Precision Training (MPT) standardizes the practice of maintaining master weights in fp32 while computing activations and gradients in fp16 or bf16 to maximize throughput and minimize memory footprint17. Because gradients naturally exhibit small magnitudes, storing them in fp16 frequently induces severe underflow—the smallest representable positive normal value in fp16 is approximately   17. MPT combats this via loss scaling, dynamically multiplying the loss by a factor    to shift the gradient distribution into the representable fp16 range, and scaling the updates back down before applying them to the fp32 master weights17.
However, loss scaling relies on a single global scalar   . The    spectral bias dictates an extreme divergence in gradient magnitudes across the network's eigen-directions3. If the global loss scale is tuned to perfectly capture the dominant gradients without inducing fp16 overflow (which occurs above 65,504), the resulting multiplier may remain entirely insufficient to elevate the infinitesimally small melody-direction gradients above the    underflow threshold.

5. Interconnected Network Confounds: Flash Attention Mechanics
The DiT is a highly interconnected network where localized precision errors do not remain isolated; they compound non-linearly. The utilization of the Flash Attention kernel introduces a uniquely destructive numerical confound directly tied to low-precision arithmetic.
5.1 Biased Rounding in Tiled Softmax
Flash Attention achieves remarkable I/O efficiency by partitioning the    attention score matrix    into hardware-cache-resident tiles23. To avoid materializing the full matrix in High Bandwidth Memory (HBM), the algorithm utilizes an online, numerically stable softmax computation. It maintains running statistics—specifically the running maximum    and the sum of exponentials   —re-scaling previously accumulated outputs as new local maximums are encountered24.
Recent mechanistic analyses of low-precision transformer failures (Qiu et al., 2025/2026) have isolated a catastrophic numerical instability within this exact online softmax routine26. When operating in bf16 or fp16, the restricted mantissa (7 bits for bf16, 10 bits for fp16) struggles with tied values. When a row of the attention score matrix contains multiple identical maximum values, the standard floating-point addition experiences a "sticky-bit" rounding phenomenon23. This results in the unnormalized outputs yielding exact    entries, introducing a systematic, strictly positive rounding bias28.
5.2 Low-Rank Collapse and Spectral Norm Explosion
This strictly positive rounding bias does not manifest as random Gaussian noise. Because attention mechanisms naturally develop similar, low-rank representations across different tokens and steps, the biased rounding errors act as coefficients that multiply these low-rank representations27.
This creates a vicious cycle of error accumulation26. The strictly positive bias continuously compounds in a dominant direction during the forward pass. During backpropagation, this highly structured error corrupts the weight updates, causing the spectral norm of the critical attention layers to artificially inflate, eventually culminating in a sudden and catastrophic loss divergence28.
For a sub-dominant signal like the 15-dimensional melody subspace, this phenomenon is lethal. The spectral norm explosion in the dominant, low-rank directions acts as overwhelming numerical noise, completely obliterating the already-fragile gradient signal associated with the melody. To mitigate this, state-of-the-art kernels employ a "safe softmax" or dynamic maximum adjustment, which dynamically adjusts the normalization factor only when a row contains multiple identical maximum values, thereby breaking the biased rounding cycle26. Evaluating whether the currently deployed Flash Attention kernel utilizes this specific mitigation is paramount.

	8. Conclusion and Strategic Implementation Directives
The challenge of representing and learning a 15-dimensional sub-dominant signal holding 9.3% variance within a 786× anisotropic latent space cannot be solved by applying global, tensor-wide precision modifications. The inverse-variance spectral law mathematically guarantees that the gradient signal required to learn this subspace will remain microscopically small, rendering it highly susceptible to the truncation limits of standard bf16/fp16 arithmetic. Furthermore, the reliance on low-precision Flash Attention introduces a structured, strictly positive rounding bias that inflates the spectral norm in dominant directions, further drowning out the sub-dominant signal.
To resolve these interconnected precision failures, the following structured interventions must be prioritized:
   1. Isolate Forward Representation Destruction: Utilize the differential signal injection protocol. Execute the synthetic-MIDI battery using both strict fp32 PyTorch attention and the production fp16 Flash Attention. Project the    outputs onto the known melody eigenbasis. If cosine similarity degrades, the forward representation is actively suppressing the signal.
   2. Mitigate Attention Kernel Bias: If the forward pass is destructive, audit the Flash Attention implementation for sticky-bit rounding vulnerabilities. Deploy "safe softmax" algorithmic variants that dynamically adjust normalization factors during row-maximum ties, breaking the low-rank error accumulation cycle.
   3. Deploy Stochastic Rounding for Parameter Updates: To solve the gradient under-investment issue dictated by the spectral bias, immediately replace the standard deterministic parameter update logic in the optimizer. Implement a custom PyTorch optimizer utilizing torch.stochastic_rounding during the master weight update phase. This transforms gradient truncation into an unbiased probabilistic distribution, ensuring the accumulation of the sub-dominant signal over successive training steps.
Standard automated precision tools are engineered to preserve dominant variance. Recognizing that these tools are fundamentally misaligned with the topological reality of the melody subspace allows the engineering architecture to pivot toward these targeted, mathematically rigorous interventions.
