# From K-Nets to PK-Nets: A Categorical Approach

**Alexandre Popoff, Carlos Agon, Moreno Andreatta, Andrée Ehresmann** ·
*Perspectives of New Music* 54(2), Summer 2016, pp. 5–63 · read 2026-08-12 (WINTERMUTE), pp. 5–7

**Verdict up front: concept-level relevance only. Not a control candidate, low direct yield.**
Filed short deliberately — there is one transferable idea and one useful warning, and padding it
out would misrepresent what it offers us.

---

## What it contains

Transformational music theory in the Lewin (1987) / Mazzola (1990) line. **Klumpenhouwer
networks (K-nets)** are directed graphs whose vertices are pitch-classes and whose edges are
elements of the T/I group (transposition/inversion), or the more general T/M affine group.

The organising idea: **describe the inner structure of a chord by the transformations *between*
its elements rather than by the elements themselves.** **Isography** is then an algebraic
relation between two K-nets that is *independent of the content of the nodes* — two chords with
entirely different pitches can be isographic if their internal transformation structure matches.

K-nets are commutative diagrams; the "path consistency condition" music theorists use is just
composition and associativity of morphisms. The paper's contribution is **PK-nets** (poly-K-nets,
valued in **Sets**), generalising K-nets to fix a specific fragility described below.

---

## What stays ours

**1 — Isography is the harmonic analogue of contour, and that is a real conceptual link to live
work.** C's morphological-space thread (Kant & Polansky) defines contour as an *equivalence
class* — transposition- and scale-invariant by construction. Isography is the same move applied
to harmonic structure: same relational shape, different absolute content. And it rhymes with C's
empirical chroma result, where **raw** chroma is inflated by the genre-generic component and only
the **demeaned** signal carries identity (air 0.918 vs raw ≈ null). Three independent routes —
contour theory, transformational theory, and a measurement on our own corpus — all landing on
*the relations carry the identity, the absolute values carry the genre*. That convergence is
worth recording even though this paper contributes no method we would run.

**2 — The transferable warning, which is the concrete thing here.** K-nets are **sensitive to how
their arrows are labelled**: the authors show two networks (Examples 1 and 2, pp. 6–7) that are
isographic under one arrow labelling and **lose isography under a different, equally valid
labelling of the same relations**. PK-nets exist to repair exactly that.

That is a caution for any relational descriptor we build — including the score-network features
from 2006.01033 and the meter-network descriptors from Yust–Popoff. **A relational
representation can be fragile to a choice that looks like bookkeeping.** If we compute
"structural similarity" between two passages from a graph whose edges we labelled by convention,
we should check that a different defensible labelling gives the same answer. That check is cheap
and nobody would think to run it without having read this.

**Why it is not a control candidate.** Against Kim's three-part test: computable from a clip —
only via symbolic chord extraction, and then only for pitch-class sets; askable by ear — no, "make
this more isographic" is not a listening request; time-series — not naturally. It fails two legs.
This is analytical apparatus for scored music, not a knob.

## Status

Read pp. 5–7 of 59 (introduction, K-net definition, isography, the labelling-sensitivity examples
that motivate PK-nets). **The PK-net construction itself (§5 onward), the categorical machinery,
and all analytical applications are unread**, and I make no claim about them.

Filed at this depth on purpose. The paper is a serious contribution to transformational theory
and would repay a full read by someone doing music analysis; for our control-target sweep the
yield is one conceptual convergence and one methodological warning, both captured above.
