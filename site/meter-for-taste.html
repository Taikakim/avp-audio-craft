A Meter for Taste

We render tens of thousands of clips and one person listens to them. This is the attempt to build a number that agrees with that person — what it took, what it costs, the one feature that looked useless and turned out to matter, and the headline result that did not survive being checked.

718 A/B votes · 1,584 ratings · 77,504 scored clips · 2026-09-07

Every training run we do produces checkpoints, and every checkpoint produces clips. There are now 77,504 of them with quality scores attached. Exactly one person's opinion decides whether a model was any good, and that person cannot listen to 77,504 clips.

So the job is a proxy: a number computed from audio that ranks clips the way the listener would. Not to replace the ear — to decide which 40 clips the ear should spend its afternoon on.

The previous version of this analysis landed on a single answer: Audiobox production quality, by itself, predicting pairwise preference at 77.6%. Every attempt to improve on it made it worse. That result has now been rebuilt on 15% more data, and it holds — as far as it goes. What has changed is that we found where it stops going.

The feature that looked useless

Audiobox reports four heads. PQ is production quality — is this well made. CU is content usefulness — is there something here worth using. Fitted as straight lines against 613 head-to-head votes, PQ predicts at 78.1% and CU at 76.3%. CU loses. In the previous report it barely appeared.

But CU kept turning up inside the better multi-feature combinations, which is a strange thing for a weak feature to do. The reason is in the shape.

The CU case, in three sentences

CU is uninformative when two clips are close and decisive when they are far apart, and its relationship to rating rises then reverses. A linear model must pick one slope for all of that, so it picks a bad compromise and CU looks like a weak feature. A model that can split on the value instead — a tree — uses CU where it works and ignores it where it doesn't, and CU becomes the single most important input.

The ensemble that didn’t survive its own cross-validation

With that established, the search is no longer over weightings. We tried those exhaustively: every one, two and three-feature combination, weighted optimally; all fourteen features under six regularisation strengths in both L1 and L2; pairwise interactions; rank transforms. Not one linear weighting beats PQ alone.

Dropping the linearity requirement appeared to change the answer. It did not. This section originally reported gradient boosting at 82.6% — a headline result that was wrong, and the reason it was wrong is more useful than the number would have been.

The stored votes are not independent comparisons. The 1020 rows are 293 distinct pairs rated on three to five questions each: the same two clips judged on different dimensions. Cross-validation grouped by row therefore puts the same pair of clips in both the training and the test fold, and a tree with enough capacity to memorise a clip pair scores itself on material it has already seen. Grouping by pair instead — the honest unit — costs the ensemble nearly eight points and drops it below the single feature it was supposed to beat.

What this leaves standing

PQ alone is still the best single meter we have for the A/B question, and the exhaustive linear search above is unaffected — a one-feature linear model has almost nothing to memorise a pair with, which is exactly why the leak inflated the tree and not the line.

The CU finding earlier in this piece also stands on its own evidence. It came from descriptive win-rate and rating curves, not from the cross-validated fit, so nothing about it depends on the ensemble. CU is non-linear in a way a straight line cannot use; that remains true and remains the more interesting observation.

The honest summary is therefore narrower than the one this page first carried: nothing we have tried beats production quality on the A/B question — not a weighting, and not a tree. The gains are on the rating question, below, where the unit of analysis is a single clip and this leak does not arise.

Why this correction is in the page rather than behind it

The error was caught by someone else re-deriving the grouping, not by the fit reporting anything unusual — an 82.6% with a clean p-value and untuned hyperparameters looks exactly like a real result. That is the whole hazard of a leaking split: it fails toward a discovery. Leaving the retraction visible is cheaper than the alternative, which is a number that keeps getting cited after it stops being true.

Two questions, two different answers

There are two human signals here and they are not the same question. A blind A/B asks which of these two. A 0–5 rating asks how good is this — and the listener has been explicit that a 4 or a 5 can go to a clip whose sound is suboptimal if the musical content is genuinely good.

The features bear that out. Predicting the ratings needs content axes that the votes do not.

On the ratings, pq + clap + flux was selected in 5 of 5 outer folds under nested cross-validation — completely stable, for an honest +0.094 R². Flux is spectral rate-of-change: how much the sound is actually moving. CLAP is semantic agreement with the prompt. Both are content measures, and both earn their place on the rating target while failing on the vote target.

That last part is worth stating plainly, because CLAP was publicly retracted from this analysis three weeks ago. That retraction was correct and still is: on pairwise preference CLAP remains worse than PQ alone, now measured on 475 matched pairs rather than 321. What is new is that the retraction was scoped to the wrong question. CLAP is redundant with production quality when you ask which of two clips is better. It is not redundant when you ask whether a clip is any good.

An aside that fell out of it: post-trained models trade variance, they don't lose it

A standing hunch on this project is that clips from the post-trained model score well because they have a punchy, clear sound, but are less varied. Measuring the spread within each checkpoint — 63 post-trained models against 237 base-rendered — says that is half right, and it is a specific half.

Where the meter stops: nothing tells a 4 from a 5

The A/B votes above ask which of two clips is better. The other signal — a 0–5 rating of a single clip — turns out to answer a sharper question, because it lets us ask where on the scale a feature is doing its work.

Production quality does almost all of its work at the bottom. Between a 1 and a 2 it is emphatic. By the top step it is not merely weak: the mean PQ of a 4 and the mean PQ of a 5 are identical to three decimals, 7.922 against 7.922. Its spread narrows the same way, from 1.00 at a 1 to 0.31 at a 5. It is a floor detector — excellent at “is this broken”, blind to “is this good”.

The obvious escape hatch is that the top rating might be reached by two opposite routes — one clean and professional, one rough but musically interesting — whose feature means would cancel. That is testable, and it is wrong: two opposing routes would make the 5s wider than the 4s, and every measured spread is narrower or equal (crest 0.64×, CU 0.76×), with no sign of a two-humped shape. The 5s are a tighter cloud sitting on top of the 4s, not two clouds.

So the gap was searched for directly, and it stayed empty:

  • Fourteen low-level audio features — loudness, brightness, dynamics, onset density, tempo, the four Audiobox heads. All flat at the top step.
  • Similarity to the training corpus, on the hunch that top marks go to clips that sound like the source material. Measured in CLAP audio-embedding space against 800 excerpts from 400 training tracks: d = 0.08 ± 0.18. Its own falsifying prediction failed too — if familiarity were being rewarded, the very closest clips should read as near-copies and score worse, and the curve is monotonic to the last decile.
  • 519 semantic classifier outputs — mood, emotion, instrumentation, 400 genres, and two regressions trained on human judgements of exactly this axis. Zero survive correction, and a permutation test settles it: the largest effect found anywhere in those 519 columns is within what 519 correlated columns produce from shuffled labels (p = 0.09). The two best-motivated heads, named in advance, were the flattest of all.

Those same 519 outputs describe the 3→4 step vividly — 45 of them survive correction, and they agree on one thing: a 4 is recognised as actually being the genre and as melodic, where a 3 reads as generic beat-driven library music. So the instruments work. They just stop one step early.

What every one of these measures has in common is that it is a summary over the whole clip. The distinction being missed was described from the listening chair as loopy and unchanging versus developing — a property of arrangement over time, which a mean across twenty seconds cannot see by construction. That is the standing hypothesis, and it is the one thing not yet measured.

The part that should make us uncomfortable

This meter is fitted to one person's ear. That is a legitimate thing to build — a studio tool does not have to generalise to be useful, and nothing here claims to measure quality in general. But it stops being only a measuring instrument the moment we rank models with it.

If checkpoint selection, best-of-N rendering and soup weighting all consult the same number, the number stops describing the project and starts steering it. Everything it cannot see gets quietly selected against, generation after generation, and the corpus drifts toward whatever the proxy rewards. The failure would not announce itself; the scores would keep going up.

Two guards seem worth keeping. Blind A/B votes have to keep arriving from outside the ranking, or the proxy ends up being validated on clips it selected. And the disagreements are the valuable part of the record: the cases where the meter says one thing and the ear says another are the only evidence that the meter has a boundary. It has one — 78.1% agreement means better than one judgment in five still goes the other way.

Limits

  • 613 pairs is a small training set for a fourteen-feature tree model. The gain is stable across 30 folds and six seeds, but this is not a large-data result.
  • The rating target is one listener, and ratings are not blind. The A/B votes are.
  • Nothing here is randomised. Every clip came from a run chosen for other reasons.
  • The degradation screen is DSP — high-frequency ratio, spectral flatness, crest — and catches buzz and whitening, not dullness, and has no measure of rhythmic integrity at all.
  • An earlier claim in this series was retracted for exactly the error this post could repeat. A three-feature model was reported at 78.8% having been scored on fewer pairs than the baseline it beat. Every comparison here is on an identical pair set, and the searched-for winners were re-checked under nested cross-validation — where the apparent +2.4 point gain from subset search collapsed to +0.8.
